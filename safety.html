Cut / Uncut

Cut:
Trimmed and dried flower. Fewer leaves and stems means less hiding space for pests or mould.
Uncut:
Whole plant or untrimmed branch. More leaf, stem and moisture, so inspect deeper for mould, mites and debris.

What sprayed can mean

PGR weed is one type, not the whole story

Sprayed is a street word, not a lab result. It usually means flower was treated after harvest to make it heavier, denser, harder, stickier or stronger looking. The tight, clumped, rock hard bud people call PGR weed is one pattern. Pesticides, synthetic cannabinoids, sugar, sand, glass, talc, rubbery additives and flavour sprays are others.

A dense bud is not automatically sprayed. Genetics, machine trimming, compression and a rushed cure can all make flower look tight. Only a lab test can prove a residue or adulterant.

The clumped bud warning

PGRs

What they are: plant growth regulators such as paclobutrazol, chlormequat, daminozide or uniconazole. They are used to control stretch and bulk up structure. Rules vary, and many are not approved for cannabis.

What people report: rock hard, smooth, clumped buds, few visible trichomes, dull orange and brown hairs, a chemical or hay smell and a harsh smoke.

Clues only. Dense structure can also come from genetics or handling.

Residue risk

Pesticides and fungicides

What they are: insecticides, miticides and mould preventatives. Some leave residues that can survive combustion.

What people report: white powder, a bitter or chemical taste, a burning throat, headache or nausea.

Ask for a pesticide panel. A smell or a photo cannot confirm it.

Emergency risk

Synthetic cannabinoids

What they are: spice or K2 type chemicals sprayed onto low grade flower to make it much stronger. The dose is unpredictable.

What people report: numbness, a chemical or solvent smell, racing heart, panic, vomiting, seizure or fainting.

Call emergency services if someone is seriously unwell. Do not smoke more.

Weight and shine

Sugar, sand, glass and rubbery additives

What they are: sugar water, Brix, syrup, hairspray, glue, sand, glass, talc, salt, chalk, oils or rubber and latex type products used to add weight or make buds look frosty and sticky.

What people report: a gritty feel, white dust, an oily film, crackling or sparks when burned, black ash and lung irritation.

Glass or sand can damage lungs. Do not use it.

Not always dangerous

Flavour and terpene sprays

What they are: natural or synthetic terpene and flavour sprays, sometimes called gas or flavour drops.

What people report: a smell much stronger than the bud looks, perfume, solvent, plastic or rubber notes, or an oily feel.

Unknown solvents can irritate lungs. A rubber or plastic smell is a red flag, not proof of one chemical.

Not the same thing

Fertilizer burn

What it is: brown, crispy leaf edges from overfeeding or salt buildup. It is a sign of plant stress, not proof of spraying.

Why it is here: the photo at the top shows fertilizer burn. It is one clue of chemical stress, not a PGR sample.

Do not use a leaf photo to diagnose PGR use.

Clumped bud check

  1. Squeeze gently. A natural bud should feel dense but springy. A rock hard, wet centred or crumbly clump is a warning sign.
  2. Look closely. Flower similar to PGR can look smooth and swollen with few trichomes and dull hairs. White powder, grit, spray droplets or an oily film are separate red flags.
  3. Smell it. Solvent, rubber, plastic, ammonia or perfume smells are reasons to stop. A hay smell can also mean a bad cure.
  4. Do not use a flame as a test. If it crackles, sparks or burns black, stop. Burning unknown residue can expose your lungs.
  5. Lab test. Pesticides, PGRs, heavy metals and synthetic cannabinoids need a lab. A photo or a squeeze cannot prove them.

If you think it has been sprayed

  • Stop smoking or vaping it. Do not give it away or sell it.
  • Keep the flower, packaging and any receipt. Note the smell, taste, time and symptoms.
  • Seek urgent medical help for chest pain, trouble breathing, seizures, fainting, a racing heart or severe vomiting.
  • Report it to your local health authority or cannabis regulator where legal.
  • Do not try to wash, cook or cure it again. You cannot remove an unknown residue at home.

This is harm reduction information, not medical advice. If you are worried about symptoms, contact a doctor or emergency service.

Quality checks

  • Trichomes
    Cloudy, milky trichomes with a little amber usually look ripe. Clear can be early. Heavy amber can mean degraded THC.
  • Pests
    Check for webbing, black specks, tiny mites or insects, especially under leaves and deep inside buds.
  • Trim
    Tight trim with few sugar leaves. Loose leaf can hide pests, mould or a rough cure.
  • Cure
    Slow dry and cure. A hay smell, harsh smoke or a damp centre can mean it was rushed.
  • Structure
    Natural buds are dense but springy. Rock hard, smooth, clumped, brittle or wet centred flower can be similar to PGR, over dried or badly handled. Structure alone is not proof.

More checks

  • Smell Fresh, strain specific aroma. Ammonia, mildew, solvent, plastic or rubber smells are red flags. A hay smell can mean a rushed cure.
  • Ash Clean grey white ash and smooth smoke are preferred. Black ash, crackling, sparks or a gritty taste can point to a bad cure or contamination. Stop if the flower sparks or pops.
  • Mould Grey fuzz, white powder, brown patches or a musty smell. Do not use mouldy flower.
  • PGR check Tight, smooth, clumped buds with few trichomes and dull hairs are commonly called PGR weed. Genetics and handling can look similar. Structure alone is not proof.
  • Residue White powder, grit, sticky sugar, an oily film or a soapy feel are reasons to stop. A lab test is the only proof.
  • Lab test Ask for pesticide, PGR, heavy metal, mould and potency testing where legal. Visual checks cannot prove safety.
